Abstract

This report presents a comprehensive, self-contained treatment of Monocular
Visual Odometry (MVO): the estimation of a camera’s 6-DoF trajectory from a
single image stream with no external range sensor. We (1) derive every core matrix
used in the pipeline element-by-element, with worked numerical examples; (2) docu-
ment the complete engineering workflow that led from literature review to a working
implementation, including the reasoning behind every design decision; (3) describe
the software architecture in full, including the responsibility, inputs, outputs, and
internal algorithmic logic of every source file and how the files communicate; (4)
formally characterize the two dominant monocular failure modes – unobservable
scale and dynamic-object contamination – and present our two novel mitigations,
an adaptive Lowe’s-ratio threshold and epipolar-distance-based dynamic outlier re-
jection; and (5) position the work relative to the published literature to establish a
concrete axis of novelty. The report is written so that every mathematical object
is expanded in full (no matrix or vector is left opaque), so that a reader unfamiliar
with epipolar geometry can follow every step.

1

Introduction

1.1 Problem Statement

Visual Odometry (VO) is the task of estimating the ego-motion of a camera-carrying
agent using only the sequence of images it captures, without wheel encoders, GPS, or an
IMU. Formally, given an ordered sequence of images {I0, I1, . . . , IN }, VO seeks to recover
the sequence of rigid-body poses {T0, T1, . . . , TN } ∈ SE(3) that describe how the camera
moved between consecutive frames, and (optionally) a sparse 3D reconstruction of the
scene that was used to obtain that estimate.

Monocular VO restricts the sensor to a single camera. This single restriction is the

source of almost every difficulty this report addresses:

• A single image is a projective (bearing-only) measurement: every 3D point along a
ray through the camera centre projects to the same pixel. Depth is fundamentally
unobservable from one image.

• Because depth is unobservable, translation between two frames can only be recov-
ered up to an unknown positive scalar (scale ambiguity). Two scenes related by a
global scale factor produce identical images.

• Because there is no external metric reference (no baseline, as in stereo, and no depth
sensor, as in RGB-D), any small per-frame scale error compounds multiplicatively
over the trajectory, producing scale drift.

1.2 Why This Problem Matters

MVO is the core perception primitive behind monocular SLAM, drone navigation in
GPS-denied environments, AR/VR head tracking, and low-cost mobile robotics, precisely
because a single camera is cheaper, lighter, and lower-power than stereo rigs, LiDAR, or
RGB-D sensors. The price of that simplicity is the scale and robustness problems analysed
in Section 7.

2 Development Workflow

Unlike a report that only presents a finished result, this section documents the actual
sequence of engineering and research steps taken, in the order they were taken, together
with the reasoning behind each transition. This is the process by which the mathematics
in Section 5 and the code in Section 6 came to exist.

Step 1. Problem scoping. We defined the exact task:

recover a metric-scale-
free camera trajectory and a sparse 3D map from an uncalibrated or pre-
calibrated monocular RGB sequence, in as close to real time as a single-
threaded Python/OpenCV pipeline allows.

Step 2. Literature review. Before writing any code we surveyed the three dominant
algorithmic families used in the VO/SLAM literature (direct, learning-based,
feature-based) — see Section 4 for the comparison table. We read founda-
tional papers (Nist´er’s five-point algorithm, Mur-Artal et al.’s ORB-SLAM,
Engel et al.’s DSO and LSD-SLAM, Forster et al.’s SVO) to understand what
had already been tried, which failure modes were already documented (scale
drift, dynamic objects, rolling shutter, photometric calibration), and which
of those failure modes were still open problems worth targeting.

Step 3. Method selection. Based on the review, we selected the feature-based
(indirect) paradigm as our foundation, since it offered the best robustness-
to-implementation-complexity ratio and the clearest mathematical pipeline
to build novel modifications on top of (see Section 4 for the full justification
and the rejected alternatives).

Step 4. Mathematical groundwork. With the paradigm fixed, we derived the
mathematical pipeline from first principles: the pinhole camera model and in-
trinsic matrix K (Section 3.1.2), the epipolar constraint and Essential matrix
E (Section 5.1), pose recovery from E via SVD (Section 5.3), triangulation
via DLT (Section 5.4), and the bundle-adjustment cost function (Section 5.5).
Each formula was validated by hand with a toy numerical example (pure lat-
eral translation, zero rotation) before being trusted in code — these worked
examples are reproduced in Section 5.

Step 5. Offline calibration. We implemented calibration.py first, since every
downstream module depends on a correct K and distortion vector D. This

4

was validated in isolation (checkerboard reprojection error < 0.5 px) before
any odometry code was written.

Step 6. Front-end (per-frame perception). We implemented feature extractor.py

(undistortion, pyramiding, FAST detection, BRIEF description) and tested
it independently by visually verifying keypoint stability across consecutive
frames.

Step 7. Two-view geometry. We implemented the matching, RANSAC + five-

point Essential-matrix estimation, and SVD-based pose recovery inside pose estimator.py,
validating each stage against the hand-derived toy example from Step 4 be-
fore integrating with real image data.

Step 8. Triangulation and map initialisation. We extended pose estimator.py
with DLT triangulation, and cross-checked the recovered 3D points against
approximate real-world distances (e.g., known checkerboard square size) to
catch scale/sign errors early.

Step 9. Backend optimisation. We implemented bundle adjuster.py using g2o,
connecting it to the front end via a shared in-memory pose/map-point buffer
maintained by main.py. We validated it by confirming that reprojection error
dropped monotonically after each optimisation call on a static test sequence.

Step 10. System integration. main.py was written last, once every module had
been validated in isolation, to orchestrate the full per-frame loop, manage
the state machine (initialisation / tracking / relocalisation), and own the
global trajectory and map buffers.

Step 11. Visualisation. visualizer.py was added purely for debugging and quali-
tative evaluation, running on a separate process so that OpenGL rendering
never blocked the tracking loop.

Step 12. Baseline error analysis. With the full pipeline running end-to-end, we
ran it on real sequences and catalogued the two catastrophic error modes
actually observed: monotonic scale drift over long sequences, and severe
rotation/translation corruption whenever a large moving object (e.g., a car)
dominated the frame. This is documented in Section 7.

Step 13. Targeted novelty. Only after the baseline failure modes were empirically
confirmed did we design targeted fixes: the adaptive Lowe’s-ratio threshold
and the epipolar-distance dynamic-object filter (Section 7.2), both imple-
mented as additions to pose estimator.py rather than as a redesign of the
pipeline, to keep the change isolated and independently ablatable.

Step 14. Comparative positioning. Finally, we compared our modified pipeline’s
qualitative and quantitative behaviour against the literature baselines iden-
tified in Step 2, to state precisely what is novel about this work and what is
not (Section 8).

5. main.py composes the relative pose onto the running trajectory (Tk = Tk−1 · [R | t]

in homogeneous form) and appends new map points to the global map.

6. Periodically (every M keyframes): main.py → bundle adjuster.py: a local
window of poses + map points in → corrected poses + map points out, which
overwrite the global buffers.

7. Continuously, on a separate process: main.py → visualizer.py: streamed
copies of the trajectory and map for rendering, decoupled so that rendering never
blocks tracking.

This makes main.py the single owner of shared state (the global trajectory list and
map-point dictionary); every other module is a stateless (or self-contained-state) function
of its inputs, which is what keeps each module independently testable, as described in
Section 2.

6.3 Module-by-Module Logic

6.3.1 calibration.py

Purpose: produce K and D (Section 3.1.2).
Inputs: a directory of checkerboard images (known real-world square size).
Outputs: camera params.yaml containing K and D.
Core logic: for every image, cv2.findChessboardCorners locates the checkerboard’s
2D grid intersections. Because the same grid’s 3D coordinates in its own reference frame
are known exactly (planar, regularly spaced, Z = 0), each image supplies a set of 2D↔3D
correspondences. Zhang’s method poses this as a non-linear least-squares problem over
all images simultaneously, solved with Levenberg-Marquardt, minimising the total repro-
jection error (the same error form as Section 5.5, but here the ”pose” being solved for is
the checkerboard’s pose relative to the camera in each image, and the free parameters are
K and D shared across all images). This module runs once, offline, before any odometry.

6.3.2 feature extractor.py

Purpose: turn a raw pixel matrix Ik into trackable, matchable numerical vectors.
Inputs: raw frame Ik, K, D.
Outputs: keypoint array (N × 2 pixel coordinates), descriptor array (N × 256 binary
vectors).
Core logic (in execution order):

1. Undistortion: applies the inverse of the distortion model in Section 3.1.2 using
K, D, so that straight lines in the world are straight in the image and the epipolar
constraint of Section 5.1 holds.

2. Image pyramids: the frame is repeatedly downsampled (e.g. by a factor of

2
per level, 4-8 levels) so the same physical corner is detectable whether the camera
is close to or far from it (scale invariance) — a feature seen at level ℓ has effective
scale 2ℓ/2 relative to the original.

3. FAST detection: for each candidate pixel, intensities on a 16-pixel Bresenham
circle around it are compared to the centre pixel ± a threshold tFAST; the pixel is a
corner if ≥ 12 contiguous circle pixels are all brighter or all darker than the centre

4. BRIEF description: around each detected corner, 256 pairs of nearby pixels (from

Returns the N × 2 keypoint-coordinate array and N × 256 descriptor array to main.py.

6.3.3 pose estimator.py

Purpose: compute the relative pose (R, t) between consecutive frames and triangulate
new 3D points; also hosts our two novel robustness additions (Section 7.2).
Inputs: keypoints/descriptors for Ik−1 and Ik.
Outputs: relative pose (R, t), newly triangulated points Xi, and a per-point inlier/dynamic-
outlier flag.
Core logic (in execution order):

1. Brute-force matching: every descriptor in frame k−1 is compared, via Hamming
distance, to every descriptor in frame k; the closest and second-closest matches are
kept for each keypoint.

2. Adaptive Lowe’s ratio test (novel, Section 7.2): a match is kept only if
dclosest/dsecond-closest < τ , where τ is adjusted per-frame based on the frame’s motion-
blur estimate.

3. Essential matrix estimation: the surviving matches, normalized by K −1, are
passed to the five-point algorithm wrapped in a RANSAC loop, which repeatedly
samples minimal sets of 5 correspondences, computes a candidate E, and counts
inliers satisfying xT
2 Ex1 ≈ 0 within a pixel-distance tolerance; the E with the most
inliers is kept.

4. Pose recovery: SVD-based decomposition of E into (R, t) as in Section 5.3, with

the cheirality check to disambiguate the four candidate solutions.

5. Strict epipolar-distance dynamic filtering (novel, Section 7.2): every in-
lier’s perpendicular distance to its epipolar line is recomputed using the final E
and compared to a strict threshold; points exceeding it are flagged as dynamic and
excluded from triangulation and from the Bundle Adjustment graph.

6. Triangulation: DLT (Section 5.4) is applied to every surviving static inlier to

produce new map points.

6.3.4 bundle adjuster.py

Purpose: suppress accumulated drift over a local window of frames (Section 5.5).
Inputs: a local window of recent poses {Tk} and map points {Xi} (excluding anything
flagged dynamic by pose estimator.py).

Outputs: corrected poses and map points.
Core logic: builds a bipartite factor graph in g2o — pose vertices in SE(3), landmark
vertices in R3, and reprojection-error edges connecting each landmark to every pose
that observed it. g2o’s Levenberg-Marquardt solver iteratively perturbs every vertex to
minimise the summed robustified reprojection error (Section 5.5) until convergence, then
the corrected values are written back to main.py’s global buffers.

6.3.5 visualizer.py

Purpose: real-time qualitative feedback (trajectory + map) for debugging and evalua-
tion.
Inputs: streamed copies of the global trajectory and map.
Core logic: because OpenGL rendering would otherwise contend with the Python GIL
and stall the main tracking loop, this module is spawned as a separate daemon process
(via multiprocessing) and communicates with main.py over an inter-process queue. It
uses Pangolin to draw camera frustums along the estimated trajectory and to plot sparse
map points as OpenGL points, updated every frame.

6.3.6 main.py

Purpose: the central execution loop, state manager, and sole owner of global shared
state.
Core logic: initialises all module objects with K, D; enters a per-frame while loop im-
plementing a three-state machine (Initialisation — bootstrap the first two keyframes
and initial map; Tracking — the steady-state per-frame loop of Section 4.2; Relo-
calisation — triggered when the inlier count from pose estimator.py drops below a
threshold, indicating tracking loss, at which point the system attempts to re-match the
current frame against recent keyframes rather than the immediately previous frame).
main.py also owns and mutates the global trajectory list and map-point dictionary that
every other module reads from or writes to.

7 Error Analysis and Our Novel Approach

7.1 Inherent Errors in the Baseline Pipeline

1. Scale drift. As shown in Section 5.3, t is recovered from the null vector u3 of E,
which is only defined up to sign and is implicitly normalised (∥t∥ = 1) by the SVD.
There is no mechanism in the base pipeline that ties this per-frame unit-norm t to a
consistent real-world scale across frames; over hundreds/thousands of frames, small
per-frame scale inconsistencies accumulate multiplicatively, warping the trajectory
(most visibly as an incorrect turning radius or an incorrect final loop-closure gap).

2. Dynamic-object contamination. Standard RANSAC assumes that most corre-
spondences are geometrically consistent with a single rigid motion. When a large
moving object (e.g., a car crossing the frame) supplies enough self-consistent cor-
respondences, RANSAC can lock onto the wrong motion hypothesis or dilute the
inlier set, corrupting the recovered R (rotation errors are visually the most damag-
ing, since they compound directionally across the whole subsequent trajectory).

14

7.2 Our Novel Mitigations

Both additions live inside pose estimator.py (Section 6.3.3) as isolated, ablatable stages
rather than as a pipeline redesign, consistent with the workflow philosophy in Section 2.

1. Adaptive Lowe’s-ratio threshold. The classical Lowe’s ratio test uses a single
fixed threshold τ (commonly 0.7–0.8) for every frame. We instead compute a per-
frame motion-blur score as the variance of the Laplacian of the (grayscale) frame,
Var(∇2Ik) — a low variance indicates a blurred, low-detail frame where descriptors
are inherently less discriminative. τ is tightened (lowered) as this variance drops,
which rejects more borderline matches exactly when they are least trustworthy,
rather than admitting a fixed proportion of matches regardless of frame quality.

√

2. Strict epipolar-distance dynamic filtering. After RANSAC converges on a
final E, we recompute, for every inlier, the perpendicular pixel distance from x2 to
its predicted epipolar line l2 = Ex1 (line in the form [a, b, c] with distance |au2 +
a2 + b2 after de-normalising by K). Points on a rigidly moving object
bv2 + c|/
satisfy a different epipolar geometry than the dominant (static-background) motion
and will systematically show larger residual distances than sensor noise alone would
produce; a strict threshold flags and permanently removes such points before they
can enter triangulation (Section 5.4) or the Bundle Adjustment graph (Section 5.5),
preventing them from corrupting either the current pose estimate or the backend
map.

Because scale drift and dynamic contamination are independent failure modes, they
are addressed independently: the ratio-test change improves match quality before E
is even estimated, while the epipolar-distance filter acts after E is estimated, cleaning
the map/BA input. Neither mitigation solves the scale-drift problem itself (which funda-
mentally requires an external metric reference such as an IMU, stereo baseline, or known
object size, and is out of scope for a purely monocular system); the report is explicit
about this limitation rather than overstating what a monocular-only fix can achieve.

8 Related Work and Positioning of Novelty

The literature already documents both failure modes we target (see the reference list be-
low): scale drift is characterised in scale-drift-aware SLAM work, and dynamic-environment
robustness is surveyed broadly in the VSLAM-in-dynamic-environments literature. Our
contribution is not the discovery of these problems, but two lightweight, self-contained
modifications to the classical indirect pipeline’s front end — an adaptive matching thresh-
old driven by a motion-blur proxy, and a post-RANSAC epipolar-distance dynamic filter
— that require no additional sensors, no learned model, and no architectural change to
the rest of the pipeline. When you populate the empirical section of your own paper,
the concrete axis of comparison should be: (a) trajectory accuracy (Absolute Trajec-
tory Error / Relative Pose Error) against ORB-SLAM2/ORB-SLAM3 and DSO on a
standard benchmark (e.g., KITTI or TUM RGB-D/TUM-Mono) with and without your
two modifications (an ablation), and (b) qualitative/quantitative robustness specifically
on sequences containing moving objects, since that is where the epipolar-distance filter
should show its largest relative improvement.
